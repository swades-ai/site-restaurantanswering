RestaurantAnswering is an AI answering service for restaurants that answers every call 24/7 — phone orders, reservations, waitlists, menu and hours questions, catering and after-hours coverage — without adding staff. The comparison that matters to a buyer is not AI against human in the abstract; it is which of your calls the agent should take, which it should hand to a person, and what the minute-based pricing does to that split.

What follows is what the system does at the call level, how it plugs into the rest of your stack, what the pricing model rewards and punishes, and the cases where a human still wins.

## What RestaurantAnswering actually does on a call

The product is a voice agent that picks up the phone line your guests already dial. It is not a web chat widget and not an IVR tree — the caller speaks in normal sentences and the agent responds in normal sentences, using your real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time.

Scope, as published by the vendor:

- Phone orders, taken to completion and pushed to the POS.
- Reservations, booked against live availability.
- Waitlists, with the caller told where they stand.
- Menu and hours questions, answered from your own data.
- Catering enquiries, captured with the details a kitchen needs.
- After-hours coverage, which is where most of the recovered revenue is claimed to sit.

Two sentences of context before that list matter more than the list itself. The first is that "answers every call 24/7" is a coverage claim, not a quality claim — a call answered at 2am by an agent that cannot see tonight's 86 list is worse than a voicemail. The second is that any accuracy figure a vendor quotes is an average, and averages hide the calls that matter: a modifier-heavy order at peak is not the same task as "what time do you close".

If your problem is specifically the calls that arrive after the kitchen closes, the [after hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) page is the more direct read than this comparison. If your problem is volume at 7pm on a Friday, read [high volume call handling](/high-volume-call-handling/) instead.

## How it connects to your POS and reservation stack

The claim that matters most for a restaurant buyer is that the system connects directly with your POS, reservation platforms and phone provider. That is the difference between an answering service and an order-taking system: an answering service writes down what the caller said and someone re-keys it; an integrated agent writes the order into the till.

What to verify before you buy, in order:

1. **POS integration depth.** Does the agent see live item availability and 86'd items, or a static menu file? A static file produces orders for dishes you ran out of at 8pm.
2. **Modifier handling.** "No onions, extra sauce, gluten free" is where order accuracy is won or lost. Ask for a demo using your ten most-modified items.
3. **Reservation platform.** If you run a waitlist and a booking book in two systems, confirm which one the agent writes to and whether the two reconcile.
4. **Phone provider.** Number porting, call forwarding on busy, and what happens when the agent fails — does the call fall through to a human or to a dead line?
5. **Payment.** Whether card details are taken on the call, and by whom.

The [integrations](/integrations/) page lists what is supported. For reservation-specific behaviour, [capture reseravations](/capture-reseravations/) covers the booking flow, and [catering management](/catering-management/) covers the larger-ticket enquiries where a misheard detail costs real money.

## Pricing model: minutes, not seats

RestaurantAnswering prices on included minutes with volume-based overage, not per seat and not per location. The published entry plan is $29/month, and the annual equivalent is $348/year.

Above the entry tier the published plans are Business at $199/month ($2350/year annually) and Pro at $450/month ($5400/year annually). Metered overage on those tiers is published at $0.15/month and $0.13/month per minute respectively. Phone numbers are billed separately at $1.50/month each, and international numbers at $3/month.

Two things a buyer should do with those numbers before signing:

- **Estimate your minutes, not your calls.** A reservation is short. A catering enquiry is not. A Friday-night order with four modifiers can run several minutes. Pull last month's call log and multiply.
- **Check what counts as a minute.** Rounding, minimum billable duration, and whether a call that drops after ten seconds is charged all change the effective rate.

If you are running several locations, the arithmetic changes: the same call volume spread across sites may or may not aggregate into a volume tier. Ask explicitly.

## Where it fits, and where it does not

The strongest fit is a restaurant whose phones overwhelm staff during peak hours — quick service, fast casual, and multi-location groups where the same call gets answered differently depending on who is on shift. [Franchise call inconsistency](/franchise-call-inconsistency/) is the clearest version of that problem, and [eliminate missing phone orders](/eliminate-missing-phone-orders/) is the clearest version of the revenue case.

Weaker fits, stated plainly:

- **Fine dining with a dedicated reservations line.** If a host already answers every call within two rings, the agent is replacing a working process. See [fine dining](/fine-dining/).
- **Menus that change daily.** The agent is only as good as the data behind it; a kitchen that rewrites the menu at 4pm needs a workflow for updating the agent too.
- **Heavy complaint handling.** [Complaint script template](/complaint-script-template/) exists because complaints need judgement, escalation and a human name at the end.
- **Allergy questions.** An agent can read your allergy script, but the liability sits with you. See [allergy script template](/allergy-script-template/).

For vertical-specific behaviour, the site publishes pages for [burger restaurant](/burger-restaurant/), [coffee shop](/coffee-shop/), [pizza-adjacent Italian](/italian-restaurant/), [Chinese restaurant](/chinese-restaurant/), [Indian restaurant](/indian-restaurant/), [Japanese restaurant](/japanese-restaurant/), [Korean restaurant](/korean-restaurant/), [Greek restaurant](/greek-restaurant/), [bakery](/bakery/), [bar](/bar/), [buffet](/buffet/), [food court](/food-court/), [food truck](/food-truck/), [ghost kitchens](/ghost-kitchens/) and [dessert restaurant](/dessert-restaurant/). | Dimension | RestaurantAnswering | A human answering service | An in-house host |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours | 24/7, per vendor | Usually 24/7, per contract | Your opening hours |
| Order entry | Written to POS via integration | Message taken, re-keyed | Written to POS |
| Reservations | Live availability | Message taken | Live availability |
| Peak-hour scaling | Concurrent calls | Depends on agent pool | One call at a time |
| Cost shape | Included minutes plus overage | Per call or per minute | Hourly wage |
| Menu knowledge | Your data, as current as you keep it | Script | Full |

Two notes on that table. The in-house host column is not a straw man — for many single-site restaurants it is still the cheapest correct answer, because the host is already on the floor. And the answering-service column is not uniformly worse: a good human service handles an angry regular better than any agent, which is why the complaint and allergy cases above sit outside the fit.

## Compliance and data handling

The vendor publishes SOC 2, HIPAA & GDPR compliance on the Enterprise plan. Read that sentence carefully: compliance is an Enterprise-plan attribute, not a property of the product at every tier. If you take card payments over the phone, or if you are part of a group with a legal team, that distinction decides which plan you are actually buying.

Questions to put in writing before signing: where call recordings are stored, how long they are retained, who can access them, whether card data is ever in scope, and what the deletion process is when you leave. None of those are answered by a compliance badge alone.

## What to test in a trial

Run the agent against your own worst calls rather than the vendor's demo script. A short trial list:

- Your ten most-modified menu items, ordered by voice.
- A reservation for a party size you cannot seat, to see what it offers instead.
- An 86'd item, to see whether the agent knows.
- A complaint, to see where it escalates.
- A call in a language your staff do not all speak.
- A dropped call, to see whether it is billed.

Analytics matter here too — [analytics call reporting](/analytics-call-reporting/) is how you find out whether the agent is actually converting calls or just answering them. Without that, you are buying coverage and hoping for revenue.

## The short version

RestaurantAnswering is a strong fit when your phones are the bottleneck and your menu data is clean enough to trust an agent with it. It is a weak fit when your calls need judgement, when your menu changes faster than your update workflow, or when a host already covers the line. The pricing model rewards high call volume with short calls and punishes long, complex ones — so estimate minutes from your own logs before you pick a tier.
