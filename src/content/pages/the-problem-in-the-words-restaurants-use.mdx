## What a missed call actually costs you

A missed call is not a lost call. It is a lost order, a lost reservation, or a lost table — and the guest rarely calls back. They dial the next restaurant on the list, and you never learn the call happened.

RestaurantAnswering's own published claim is that recovering missed and after-hours calls is worth **$30k+ in annual revenue**. That figure is the vendor's, not an audited benchmark, but the arithmetic behind it is easy to check against your own call log: count the calls that rang out during Friday dinner service, multiply by your average ticket, and multiply again by 52.

### The calls you never see

The ones that hurt most are invisible in the moment:

- Calls that arrive while every staff member is on the floor, at the fryer, or at the till.
- Calls after close, when the voicemail greeting plays and the guest hangs up.
- Calls during a rush when the line is engaged and the caller gets a busy signal.
- Calls on a second line nobody was assigned to answer.

None of these show up as a complaint. They show up as a quieter Tuesday.

## Why the problem survives every fix you try

Most restaurants have already tried something. The fixes fail for structural reasons, not for lack of effort.

### Adding staff does not scale to the peak

Hiring for the phone means hiring for the busiest twenty minutes of the day and paying for the other nine hours and forty. During a genuine rush, the same person you hired to answer is the person needed on the line, at the pass, or at the counter. The phone loses that contest every time.

### Voicemail converts almost nothing

A guest ordering dinner will not leave a message and wait for a callback. A guest asking whether you have a table at seven will not leave a message either. Voicemail is a record that you missed the call, not a recovery mechanism. If you want the greeting itself to do more work, our [after hours voicemail script template](/after-hours-voicemail-script-template/) covers what to say.

### Answering services do not know your menu

A traditional answering service can take a message. It cannot tell a caller that the kitchen is out of the short rib, that the prix fixe ends at nine, or that the patio is first-come. Without your live menu, modifiers and availability, the message it takes is often wrong — and a wrong order is worse than a missed one.

### The after-hours gap is the widest

After close, the phone still rings. Catering enquiries, large-party bookings, next-day reservations and simple hours questions all arrive when nobody is there. Our [after hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) page covers that window specifically.

## What good looks like

A phone system that works for a restaurant has four properties, and they are worth stating plainly because most tools have only some of them.

1. **It answers every call 24/7** — during service, between services, and after close.
2. **It answers fast.** RestaurantAnswering claims under one second, because a caller who waits four rings has already started dialling elsewhere.
3. **It knows the menu.** Real items, real prices, real modifiers, real availability, pulled at the moment of the call.
4. **It writes into the systems you already run** — the POS for orders, the reservation book for tables, the waitlist for walk-ins.

Anything short of that is a message-taking service with better marketing.

## Deciding whether this is your problem

Pull one week of call data before you evaluate anything. Count inbound calls, count answered calls, and count calls that arrived outside posted hours. If the gap between the first two numbers is more than a handful per day, the revenue is already leaving.

Then check the third number. After-hours volume is the part most operators have never measured, and it is usually the part that justifies the change on its own.
