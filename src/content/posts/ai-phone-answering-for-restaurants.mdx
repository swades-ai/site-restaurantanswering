AI phone answering for restaurants is software that picks up your line, holds a conversation with the caller, and finishes the task — a to-go order, a reservation, a waitlist entry, or a question about hours and menu. RestaurantAnswering is one such service: it answers every call 24/7, covering phone orders, reservations, waitlists, menu and hours questions, catering and after-hours calls, without adding staff.

The category exists because of timing. Your phone rings hardest exactly when nobody can reach it — the dinner rush, the Friday night, the two hours after close. Each of those calls is a customer who has already decided to spend money with you and is now deciding whether to wait, call back, or order somewhere else.

## What the agent does on a live restaurant line

A voice agent is not an IVR menu, and the difference decides whether your callers stay on the line. An IVR pushes buttons and routes; a voice agent holds a conversation, absorbs the caller's interruption, and completes the transaction.

On a restaurant line the finished transaction is usually one of five things:

- A to-go or pickup order, read back for confirmation and sent to the kitchen or POS.
- A reservation, checked against real availability and written into the reservation platform.
- A waitlist entry with party size and a callback number.
- A factual answer — hours, address, whether you have a patio, whether the kitchen is still open.
- A catering or large-party enquiry, captured with date, headcount and contact details for a manager to call back.

Response time is the number that decides whether the system is usable on a live line, and it is a configuration question as much as a technology one. A caller who waits four seconds assumes the line is dead. Test it yourself on a mobile connection, not on the vendor's demo line.

### The calls it is built to catch

Most restaurants already know which calls they lose, and the pattern repeats across quick service, fast casual and multi-location groups. Peak-hour calls ring out while staff are on the floor; after-hours calls hit voicemail and never convert.

Pull your call log for the last month, count the calls that rang longer than 20 seconds or landed in voicemail, and multiply by your average ticket. The arithmetic on your own missed calls is the only forecast that matters, and it takes about ten minutes. The [eliminate missing phone orders](/eliminate-missing-phone-orders/) page walks through the same calculation.

### What it does not do

It does not replace your POS, your reservation book or your staff — it writes into them. It also does not handle a caller disputing a charge from three weeks ago; that call should route to a human, and any system you buy should let you define which calls escalate. The [complaint script template](/complaint-script-template/) shows how narrow those escalation categories usually are.

## How it connects to your POS and reservation platform

The value of a voice agent collapses if it reads from a stale menu. Restaurant voice AI that connects directly with your POS, reservation platforms and phone provider, using real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time, is the version worth buying. Anything that requires you to maintain a second copy of your menu will drift out of date within a month.

Ask three questions before you sign:

1. Which POS systems are supported natively, and which need middleware?
2. When I 86 an item at 7pm, how fast does the agent stop selling it?
3. When a reservation is booked by phone, does it appear in the same book my host stand uses?

RestaurantAnswering lists POS, reservation platform and phone provider connections on its [integrations](/integrations/) page. If your stack is not listed, ask what the workaround is rather than assuming one exists.

### Menu changes, modifiers and specials

Modifiers are where voice ordering usually breaks. "No onions, extra sauce, dressing on the side" is three separate changes to one line item, and a system that only reads item names fails this test in week one. Run a live test with your ten most-modified items before you commit — order each by phone exactly as a customer would, then check what lands in the kitchen.

### Phone numbers and call routing

You can keep your existing number and forward it, or take a new one. RestaurantAnswering prices phone numbers separately from minutes at $1.50/month each, so a multi-location group pays per line. Decide early whether each location gets its own number, because a shared number makes per-location call data meaningless.

### How to estimate your own minute usage

Do this before you look at any plan, because it decides which tier you land in. The estimate takes ten minutes and saves you from buying the wrong tier twice.

- Count your calls per day, split peak and off-peak.
- Estimate average call length. An order is typically longer than a reservation; a reservation is longer than an hours question.
- Multiply calls by average minutes, then by 30.
- Add headroom for seasonal peaks, because December is not February.

A restaurant taking 40 calls a day at an average of two minutes uses roughly 2,400 minutes a month, well past the Starter allowance. The question then becomes whether Business or Pro is cheaper than Starter plus overage — run both numbers, because the overage rate and the next tier's included minutes rarely line up the way you expect.

### Where the bill surprises people

Three places. Calls that run long because the agent keeps asking the caller to repeat themselves. Calls that should have been a text message. And multi-location groups that buy one number and then cannot tell which store's calls are which.

## After-hours coverage, waitlists and catering

The after-hours window is the easiest place to justify the spend, because there is no staff cost to compare it against — nobody is there. A call at 10:40pm that books a table for Saturday is pure addition to the week.

### Waitlists

A waitlist entry is short: name, party size, phone number, and how long the caller will wait. The failure mode is a host stand that cannot see phone entries alongside walk-ins, so whatever you buy must write into the same list your hosts use or you have created a second queue. The [high no show solution](/high-no-show-solution/) page covers what to do with the entries once you have them.

### Catering and large parties

Catering calls are the highest-value calls most restaurants receive and the most likely to be missed, because they arrive during service. These should not be booked by the agent — they should be captured completely and routed to a manager: date, headcount, budget indication, contact number, and delivery or pickup. Call recordings contain card numbers, addresses and phone numbers, so if you take payment over the phone the compliance question is not optional. Ask where recordings are stored, how long they are retained, and whether recording can be disabled for calls that involve payment details.

### Questions that separate a demo from a deployment

A demo runs on a clean menu and a quiet room; a deployment runs on your menu during a Friday rush. The questions below are the ones that surface the difference before you have signed anything.

- Where are calls recorded and stored, and for how long?
- Can I turn recording off for specific call types?
- Who owns the transcript data?
- What happens to a call when the agent cannot understand the caller?
- Can I listen to live calls during the first month?

### The escalation path

Every voice agent needs a defined handoff. Decide now which calls transfer to a human — complaints, allergy questions, anything involving a refund — and confirm the system supports that rule before you buy. ## How to run a trial that tells you something

A two-week trial with no measurement tells you nothing, so set the test up before the first call arrives. The sequence below isolates one variable at a time instead of turning everything on at once.

1. Forward only your after-hours line for week one. Compare booked reservations against the previous month.
2. Add the peak-hour overflow line in week two. Measure how many calls the agent completes without escalation.
3. Order your ten most-modified items by phone and check the kitchen tickets.
4. Count misheard items. A pattern of errors on simple items is a configuration problem, not a technology limit.
5. Check that every reservation the agent booked appears in your host stand's book.

If the agent cannot clear step five, stop there. Everything else is secondary to the booking landing in the right place.
