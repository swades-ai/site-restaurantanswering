## The situation: the phone rings during the rush

It is 6:40pm on a Friday. Two servers are on the floor, one is running food, the host is seating a four-top, and the phone rings for the third time in ten minutes. Nobody picks up. The caller wants to know whether you have a table for six at 7:30, or whether the kitchen can still do a large pepperoni for pickup. They hang up and call the place two blocks over.

That is the use case this page covers: the calls your team cannot physically reach, and what changes when something else answers them. The pattern repeats across [quick service counters](/burger-restaurant/), [coffee shops](/coffee-shop/) with a single register, and [multi-location groups](/franchise-call-inconsistency/) where every store handles the phone differently.

## What happens today, without an answering layer

Most restaurants run one of three setups, and each one leaks calls in a different place.

- **Nobody answers.** The line rings out during peak. The caller does not leave a voicemail, because ordering food by voicemail is not a thing people do.
- **A staff member answers.** A server leaves the floor, takes the order on a notepad, walks it to the kitchen, and returns to a table that has been waiting. The order is usually right. The service on the floor is not.
- **An after-hours message plays.** Calls that arrive after close get a recording. A recording cannot take a catering enquiry for next Saturday or add a name to tomorrow's waitlist.

[After hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) is where the loss is easiest to see, because there is no staff cost to blame it on — the calls simply never convert. The same applies to [missing phone orders](/eliminate-missing-phone-orders/) during the lunch window, when the phone and the counter compete for the same pair of hands.

### Orders

The agent reads back the items, modifiers and total, then pushes the order to the POS the same way an online order arrives. Your kitchen sees one ticket format, not two.

### Reservations and waitlists

Bookings land in your reservation platform with party size, time and contact number. Walk-in waitlist additions go to the same list your host uses, so nobody is maintaining a second spreadsheet. See [capture reservations](/capture-reseravations/) for how the booking flow is configured.

### Questions that do not need a human

Hours, address, parking, allergen questions, whether you take large parties, whether the patio is dog-friendly. These are the calls that interrupt service most often and convert least. [Allergy script template](/allergy-script-template/) covers how allergen answers are constrained so the agent never guesses.

### Catering and after-hours

Catering enquiries are the highest-value calls a restaurant gets and the ones most likely to arrive when nobody is at the desk. [Catering management](/catering-management/) handles the intake; [after hours voicemail script template](/after-hours-voicemail-script-template/) is the fallback for anything the agent routes to a human the next morning.

The strongest fit is a restaurant whose phone volume is predictable and painful: a [high-volume call operation](/high-volume-call-handling/) with a lunch rush, a [ghost kitchen](/ghost-kitchens/) with no front of house at all, or a [food truck](/food-truck/) where the person cooking is the person who would otherwise answer.
