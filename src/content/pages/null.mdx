## What a restaurantanswering comparison should settle first

A restaurantanswering comparison comes down to two questions: what happens on a call, and what you pay per minute once the included minutes run out. RestaurantAnswering is an AI answering service for restaurants that answers every call 24/7, takes phone orders, books reservations and manages waitlists without adding staff. That single sentence is the whole product surface — everything below is about how it is metered, what it connects to, and where it stops being the right tool.

The reason the comparison matters is that most restaurants do not lose calls because nobody cares. They lose them because the phone rings during the rush, the host is seating a table, and the line goes to voicemail that nobody checks until the next morning. A comparison that only looks at monthly headline price misses the minutes where the money actually is.

## The call types that decide your plan

Not every call costs the same in staff time, and not every call needs the same handling. A reservation request is a short exchange with a date, a time and a party size. A phone order is longer: items, modifiers, sides, a pickup time, sometimes a card. A menu question is thirty seconds. A catering enquiry is a lead that needs a callback, not a resolution.

RestaurantAnswering answers every call 24/7 across those types, which is why the plan you pick should be driven by your call mix rather than by your table count. A quick service location with heavy phone-order volume burns minutes faster than a fine dining room that mostly takes reservations. If your volume is concentrated in a two-hour window, look at [high volume call handling](/high-volume-call-handling/) before you look at plan tiers.

Two things follow from that. First, estimate your peak-hour call count, not your daily average, because that is what determines whether the system keeps up. Second, decide in advance which calls should end in a booking or an order and which should end in a message to your team.

## How the plans are structured

RestaurantAnswering prices on a base monthly plan plus metered minutes beyond what the plan includes. The entry point is $29, billed monthly, with a yearly option at $348/year. The mid tier is $199 per month, or $2350/year yearly. The top tier is $450/month per month, or $5400/year yearly, and there is an agency plan at $1500/month monthly or $16800/year yearly for groups running multiple locations.

Extra minutes are metered per plan. Starter overage runs at $0.15/month per minute, Business at $0.13/month, and the higher tiers at $0.12/month. Phone numbers are billed separately at $1.50/month, and international numbers at $3/month.

The practical consequence: two restaurants with the same plan can have very different bills, and the difference is call length, not call count. A location that takes full phone orders will outspend a location that only books tables, even at identical call volume. That is not a flaw in the model, but it does mean you should model your own minutes before choosing a tier rather than picking the middle option by default.

## What it connects to, and why that changes the bill

RestaurantAnswering connects directly with your POS, reservation platforms and phone provider, using real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time. That connection is what keeps calls short. If the system can see that the kitchen is out of an item, it does not spend ninety seconds taking an order for it and then have your staff call the guest back.

The integration list is the part of a comparison that is easiest to check and most often skipped. Ask specifically whether your POS is supported, whether your reservation platform is supported, and whether your existing phone number can be ported or forwarded. The [integrations](/integrations/) page is the place to confirm that before you commit, and [capture reseravations](/capture-reseravations/) covers what reservation capture looks like end to end.

Where the connection is missing, the system still answers, but it answers with less information, and less information means longer calls and more callbacks. That is a cost you will see in metered minutes rather than on the plan line.

## Where it fits, and where it does not

It fits restaurants whose phones overwhelm staff during peak hours — quick service, fast casual and multi-location groups especially. It fits after-hours coverage, where the alternative is a voicemail nobody returns. It fits catering enquiries that arrive while the kitchen is busy, and it fits groups that want the same answers at every location instead of whatever the newest hire remembers.

It does not fit every restaurant. If your call volume is a handful a day and your host has time to answer, the base plan is more than the problem costs. If your calls are mostly complex complaints that need a manager's judgement, an AI that resolves them is the wrong shape — you want routing and escalation, not resolution. And if your menu changes daily in ways your POS does not reflect, the system will be working from stale information until that is fixed.

A fair comparison therefore includes the cost of the calls you are currently missing, not just the cost of the plan. If you are not sure what you are missing, start with [after hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) and [eliminate missing phone orders](/eliminate-missing-phone-orders/), which describe the two failure modes that show up most often.

## What to test before you commit

Run a trial against your real menu, not a demo menu. The failure modes that matter — a modifier the system does not know, a reservation slot it offers when the room is full, a catering enquiry it treats as a takeaway order — only appear with your actual data.

Test in this order. First, call your own number after close and see whether the after-hours path is right. Second, place a phone order with three modifiers and check what lands in the POS. Third, ask for a reservation at a time you know is full. Fourth, ask a menu question about an item you are out of. Fifth, ask something the system should not answer and confirm it escalates rather than guesses.

Two sentences of context before the list, and one after. The point of the sequence is that each test exercises a different integration, so a failure tells you which connection to fix rather than that the product is wrong. Once those five pass, your remaining risk is volume, which you can only test by letting it run through a real Friday.

## Compliance and data handling

RestaurantAnswering carries SOC 2, HIPAA & GDPR compliance on the Enterprise plan. If you take payment details over the phone, or if you operate in a jurisdiction with strict data rules, confirm which of those applies to your plan before you go live, because the compliance posture is tied to the tier rather than to the product as a whole.

For most independent restaurants this is not the deciding factor. For groups with locations in multiple countries, or for anyone handling card data on the call, it is the first question rather than the last. Ask it in writing and keep the answer.
