## What this calculator works out

A restaurant answering cost calculator does one job: it turns your call volume into a monthly bill. You put in how many calls arrive, how long they last, and which plan tier you would sit on. It puts out a monthly figure, an overage figure, and a rough payback against what those calls cost you now.

The interactive version of this page is added by an engineer. Everything below is the arithmetic it runs, written out, so you can do it on a napkin or in a spreadsheet before you talk to anyone.

## The inputs you need before you start

Four numbers drive the whole result, and you probably already have three of them.

- **Calls per month.** Pull this from your phone provider's bill, not from memory. Peak-season weeks and quiet weeks differ enough that a single busy Friday will mislead you.
- **Average call length in minutes.** Order calls, reservation calls and menu questions run different lengths. If you can only get one figure, use the blended average from your provider.
- **Share of calls that need a person.** Allergy questions, large catering enquiries and complaints are the ones most restaurants want escalated.
- **Your current cost per answered call.** Either staff hours spent on the phone, or a per-call rate from an answering service.

Write these down before you compare anything. A calculator fed guesses produces a guess.

## The formula, in words

Monthly cost equals the base plan price, plus any minutes beyond the included allowance multiplied by the per-minute overage rate, plus the cost of any extra phone numbers.

Included minutes are the allowance that comes with the plan. Overage is what you pay once you pass it. If your plan includes a set number of minutes and you use fewer, you pay the base price and nothing more — unused minutes do not roll into a credit on most plans, so an oversized plan is wasted money.

Extra phone numbers matter for multi-location groups and for anyone running a separate line for catering or a second brand. Each number is billed per month on top of the plan.

## Worked example: a single fast-casual site

Suppose a fast-casual restaurant takes 900 calls a month at an average of 2.5 minutes. That is 2,250 minutes.

If the plan's included allowance covers 2,250 minutes, the monthly cost is the base plan price and nothing else. If the allowance is 2,000 minutes, the extra 250 minutes are billed at the plan's overage rate.

Run the same sum for a site taking 2,400 calls at 3 minutes — 7,200 minutes — and the overage line becomes the dominant part of the bill. That is the point at which a higher tier usually beats paying overage, and it is the comparison the calculator is built to make visible.

## Worked example: a three-location group

Three sites, each taking 1,500 calls a month at 2 minutes, is 9,000 minutes total. Multiply the per-number charge by the number of lines you need, then add the plan.

Groups usually find the arithmetic splits two ways: one plan covering all locations, or one plan per location. One plan is cheaper on paper until a single busy site pushes the whole account into overage, which then bills at the same rate for every location. Per-location plans cap that exposure but multiply the base price. The calculator shows both columns so you can see which side of the line you sit on.

## Where the minutes actually go

Overage is rarely caused by order volume alone. It is caused by call types that take longer than you assumed.

Menu and hours questions are short. Reservations are medium. Catering enquiries, allergy questions and complaints are long, and they are the calls most likely to need a person. If a fifth of your calls are in that last group, your average call length is not the number you think it is.

This is also where an answering service earns its place rather than just costing money. Calls that arrive during peak service are the ones your staff cannot take, and those are the calls that turn into missed orders. Our service is built to [eliminate missing phone orders](/eliminate-missing-phone-orders/) by picking up when the line is busy, and it [answers every call 24/7](/after-hour-call-handling/) rather than only during trading hours.

## Payback: what you are comparing against

The honest comparison is not the plan price against zero. It is the plan price against what you already spend answering those calls.

Two costs sit on the other side of the ledger. The first is staff time: every minute a server or host spends on the phone is a minute not spent on the floor, and that cost is real even when it does not appear as a line item. The second is missed calls — orders and reservations that never got placed because nobody picked up.

If your current arrangement is a human answering service, compare its per-call or per-minute rate against the plan plus overage. If it is staff, compare against the hours. If it is voicemail, compare against the value of the calls you lose, which is the hardest number to estimate and usually the largest.

## What the plan tiers change

The tiers differ in three ways: the included minute allowance, the overage rate, and which integrations and compliance features are switched on.

Higher tiers carry a lower overage rate and a larger allowance, so the crossover point is where your monthly minutes exceed the lower tier's allowance by enough that the overage bill exceeds the price difference between tiers. Below that point, the cheaper tier wins. Above it, the higher tier does.

Integration depth is the other variable. Our service [connects directly with your POS](/integrations/), reservation platforms and phone provider, using real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time. If your POS is not on the supported list, the calculator's numbers still hold but the order-taking part of the service does not, so check that first.

Compliance features sit on the top tier. SOC 2, HIPAA & GDPR compliance is available on the Enterprise plan, which matters if you handle payment data or health-related information in call notes.

## Reading the result

A monthly figure on its own tells you very little. Read it three ways.

First, as a per-call cost: divide the monthly total by calls answered. That number is comparable across plans, across providers and against staff time.

Second, as a share of revenue. A per-call cost of a few cents against an average order value is a different proposition from the same figure against a reservation that may or may not turn into a table.

Third, as a peak-hour cost. The value of answering the tenth call in a busy hour is higher than the value of answering the first, because the tenth is the one that would otherwise go unanswered. If your call volume is concentrated, weight the calculation toward those hours rather than spreading it evenly across the month.

## What this page does not do

It does not quote you a price. Plan pricing is set out in the price book and shown on the pricing page; the calculator uses whatever tier you select.

It does not know your POS, your reservation platform or your phone provider. Those determine whether order-taking and reservation-booking work at all, so confirm them before you trust a saving.

It does not estimate the revenue from recovered calls. That depends on your average order value and your conversion rate, both of which you know better than any calculator does.

## Next steps

If the result is close to what you already pay, the decision is about coverage — whether calls get answered at 9pm and during the Friday rush — rather than about cost.

If the result is clearly lower, check the integration list and the compliance tier before you commit, because those are the two things that change what the service can actually do for you.
