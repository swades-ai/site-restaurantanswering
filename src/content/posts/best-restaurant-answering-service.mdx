The best restaurant answering service is the one that answers every call 24/7 without adding staff, and that connects directly with your POS so orders, reservations and waitlists land in the systems you already run. Voice quality, dashboards and contract length are secondary to those two tests.

Most restaurants do not lose money because a host is rude. They lose it because the phone rings during the rush, nobody picks up, and the caller orders somewhere else. A service that only covers overflow during business hours solves half the problem; after-hours and peak-hour calls are where the recoverable revenue sits.

## What separates a restaurant answering service from a generic call centre

A generic answering service takes a message. A restaurant answering service takes an order. The difference shows up in the transcript: item names, modifiers, sides, allergy notes, pickup time, and whether the total the caller heard matches the total the kitchen sees.

Ask any vendor three questions before a demo. First, does the agent read from your live menu with current pricing and sold-out items, or from a static script you upload monthly? Second, when a caller changes their mind mid-order, does the system update the ticket or start a new one? Third, what happens when the caller asks something the system cannot answer — is there a warm transfer to a human, and to whom?

### Order accuracy is the only metric that matters at the till

Accuracy is measured at the POS, not in the call recording. A system that transcribes perfectly but writes the wrong modifier costs you a remake, a refund and a bad review. RestaurantAnswering publishes an order accuracy figure and a sub-second response time; treat both as vendor claims to verify with your own menu, not as guarantees.

Run a two-week pilot with your ten most-modified items — the ones with add-ons, swaps and allergy notes. If the tickets come through clean, the accuracy claim holds for your menu. If they do not, no dashboard will save the relationship.

### Integration decides whether the service saves labour or adds it

A service that emails you a PDF of each order has moved the work, not removed it. Someone still retypes the ticket into the POS during the busiest hour of the day. RestaurantAnswering connects directly with your POS, reservation platforms and phone provider, using real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time.

Before you sign, list every system the vendor claims to support and confirm each one against your actual stack — POS version, reservation platform, and whether your phone number can be ported or forwarded. See the [integrations](/integrations/) page for the current list.

## Coverage: what 24/7 actually means for a restaurant

"24/7" is easy to print on a pricing page. The useful question is what the service does at 11pm on a Friday when a caller wants a table for six tomorrow, and at 7pm on a Saturday when the kitchen is slammed and the phone will not stop.

RestaurantAnswering answers every call 24/7 — phone orders, reservations, waitlists, menu and hours questions, catering and after-hours coverage — without adding staff. That scope matters because the calls that go unanswered are rarely the simple ones.

### Peak-hour overflow

Peak-hour calls are the ones your host cannot take, because the host is seating people. A service that only handles after-hours calls leaves your busiest window exposed. Look for a vendor that can take overflow during service and full coverage after close on the same number.

### After-hours and closed days

After-hours callers are often the highest-intent ones: someone planning a large order, a private event, or a reservation for a date you are fully booked. A voicemail box converts almost none of them. A scripted answering flow that captures the details and confirms by text converts more. Our [after hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) page covers the flow, and the [after hours voicemail script template](/after-hours-voicemail-script-template/) is a starting point if you are still deciding.

### Waitlists and reservations

Waitlist management is where restaurants lose the most goodwill per minute. A caller who is told "we'll text you" and never hears back does not call again. Reservation capture needs to write into your booking platform, not a separate spreadsheet. See [capture reservations](/capture-reseravations/) for how that handoff works.

## Pricing models compared

Restaurant answering services price in three ways: per minute, per call, and flat monthly with an included minute pool. Per-minute punishes long orders. Per-call punishes short questions. Flat pools are predictable until you exceed them, and then the overage rate decides your bill.

### How to estimate your own minute volume

Count your calls for one week, then multiply by the average call length. A phone order averages longer than a reservation; a menu question averages under a minute. If your average is two minutes and you take 400 calls a month, you need roughly 800 minutes — well past the Starter pool, so price the Business tier and the overage rate together.

### What the revenue claim is worth to you

RestaurantAnswering states the service recovers revenue from missed and after-hours calls. That figure is the vendor's own claim, not a benchmark you can assume. The useful version for your restaurant is arithmetic you do yourself: missed calls per week, times your average ticket, times the share that would have ordered elsewhere.

## Accuracy, latency and what to test in a pilot

Response time and accuracy are the two numbers a buyer can actually verify. Sub-second pickup matters because callers hang up fast; order accuracy matters because a wrong ticket is worse than no ticket.

### Build a test menu

Load your real menu, including the items that generate the most questions: substitutions, half portions, allergen swaps, combo rules. Then place twenty calls yourself, mixing order types, and check every ticket against what you said.

### Test the edge cases

Call after close. Ask for something sold out. Ask a question the system cannot answer and see where the transfer goes. Ask for a reservation on a fully booked night. These calls tell you more than a demo script ever will.

### Check the reporting

Call reporting should show you volume by hour, call type, conversion and where calls failed. If the dashboard only shows total minutes, you cannot find the leak. See [analytics call reporting](/analytics-call-reporting/) for the fields worth demanding.

## Compliance and data handling

If you take payment over the phone or store caller details, the vendor's compliance posture is your exposure. RestaurantAnswering lists SOC 2, HIPAA & GDPR compliance on the Enterprise plan — note that these are Enterprise-tier, so confirm which tier you are buying before you assume coverage.

Ask where call recordings are stored, how long they are retained, and whether card data is ever spoken to the agent or captured by keypad. If you take catering deposits by phone, that answer determines your PCI scope.

## Matching the service to your restaurant type

Different formats break in different places. A high-volume quick-service location needs order throughput; a fine-dining room needs reservation etiquette and a human-sounding handoff; a multi-location group needs consistent answers across every store.

- Quick service and fast casual: order accuracy and peak-hour overflow matter most.
- Fine dining: reservation capture, waitlist etiquette and tone.
- Multi-location groups: identical answers across stores, plus per-location reporting.
- Ghost kitchens and food trucks: after-hours order capture with no dine-in assumptions.

## Questions to ask before you sign

A short list, in the order that protects you most.

1. Can I hear a recording of a real order on a menu like mine?
2. Which POS and reservation systems are live integrations, not roadmap items?
3. What is the overage rate, and does it change with volume?
4. Who answers when the AI cannot, and how fast?
5. What is the cancellation notice period?
6. Where are recordings stored and for how long?

Get answers in writing. A vendor that will not commit to an overage rate in the contract will change it later.

## Where this fits against hiring

A part-time host to cover phones costs more per hour than most answering plans cost per month, and still cannot cover 2am. The comparison is not answering service versus nothing; it is answering service versus the calls you currently miss plus the staff hours you currently spend on the phone.
