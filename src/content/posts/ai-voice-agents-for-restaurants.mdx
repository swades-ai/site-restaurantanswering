## What an AI voice agent does on a restaurant phone line

A voice agent is not an IVR menu and it is not a voicemail box. It holds a conversation, handles interruptions, and reads the order back to the caller before hanging up.

The scope RestaurantAnswering publishes is broad: it answers every call 24/7 — phone orders, reservations, waitlists, menu and hours questions, catering and after-hours coverage — without adding staff. Those are the four call types that dominate a restaurant line: a to-go order, a table request, a question about whether you are open, and a catering inquiry that needs a callback.

What separates a usable agent from a demo is what happens after the call ends. An order that lands in a text message to the manager is a manual re-entry job at 7pm on a Friday. An order that lands in the POS as a ticket is dinner service. Ask any vendor which one you get.

- Order capture: item, quantity, modifiers, pickup time, name and phone number
- Reservations: party size, date, time, and a confirmation the caller hears
- Waitlist: name, party size, quoted wait, and a text when the table is ready
- Questions: hours, address, parking, allergen and menu-item availability
- Escalation: transfer to a human when the caller asks, or when the agent cannot resolve it

## How it connects to your POS and reservation stack

The claim that matters most in a buying decision is whether the agent connects directly with your POS, reservation platforms and phone provider, using real menu items, pricing, modifiers and availability in real time.

That sentence is doing a lot of work. "Real time" means the agent reads your live menu, not a PDF someone uploaded in March. If an item is 86'd at 8:40pm, the agent should not sell it at 8:45pm. If your POS integration is one-way — the agent writes orders in but never reads availability back — you will oversell items during exactly the rush you bought the tool for.

Integration depth is where restaurant voice AI projects stall. The [integrations page](/integrations/) is the place to check which POS, reservation and telephony systems are supported today rather than on a roadmap. For a multi-location group, the harder question is whether each location's menu, pricing and hours are configured separately, because a shared menu across sites produces wrong prices at the register.

Two practical tests before you sign:

1. Ask the vendor to place a live order against your actual menu, including one item with a modifier and one item that is out of stock.
2. Ask what happens when the POS API is down for ten minutes during service — does the agent take a message, or does it fail silently?

## Where the missed-call cost comes from, and how to check it

The cost of missed and after-hours calls is something you can measure yourself rather than take from a vendor. Pull three months of call logs from your phone provider. Count the calls that rang out, hit voicemail, or arrived outside opening hours. Multiply by your average ticket. Most independent restaurants find the missed-call count is higher than they expected and the average ticket is lower than a vendor's model assumes — the two errors partly cancel.

The number that decides the purchase is narrower: how many of those missed calls were orders or bookings you could have captured. A missed call from a supplier or a job applicant is not revenue. If your phone provider does not log abandoned calls, that is the first thing to fix, and [analytics call reporting](/analytics-call-reporting/) is where the agent's own reporting fits in.

For restaurants where the phone rings off the hook at peak, the more useful comparison is against the cost of the alternative: an extra host or cashier dedicated to phones. That is a wage line, not a software line, and it does not cover 2am.

## Latency and the failure modes to test

Latency is the one callers notice. A pause longer than a second or so reads as a bad connection, and callers hang up or talk over the agent. Ask for a live demo on a mobile connection, not a desk phone on office wifi.

Accuracy is the one that costs money. Every order that needs correction is a refund, a remake, or a bad review. The failure modes worth testing directly:

- Heavy accents and noisy kitchens — does the agent ask for a repeat or guess?
- Modifiers — "no onions, extra sauce on the side" — does it capture both?
- Item names that sound alike on your menu
- Callers who change their mind halfway through the order
- Callers who ask for a human immediately

Ask the vendor what their accuracy figure is measured against: word-level transcription, or the final ticket matching what the caller intended. The second is the one that matters and the one vendors rarely publish.

## Compliance, data and what the Enterprise tier covers

The published compliance position is SOC 2, HIPAA & GDPR compliance on the Enterprise plan. That phrasing is precise: those certifications sit on the top tier, not on the entry plan.

If you take catering orders that include customer names, phone numbers and payment details, you are handling personal data whether or not you think of yourself that way. Ask where call recordings are stored, how long they are retained, and whether you can turn recording off for calls that include payment card details. PCI exposure on a phone order is real, and an agent that records card numbers spoken aloud creates a problem your POS does not.

For a single-location restaurant, the entry tier's data handling is usually adequate. For a multi-location group or a franchise system, the Enterprise compliance set is the tier to price, and you should ask for the report rather than the badge. ## When a voice agent is the wrong purchase

A voice agent is a bad fit if your calls are already answered, if your volume is low, or if your menu changes daily in ways no integration can track.

It is also a bad fit if you cannot get the POS integration working. A voice agent that takes orders into a separate dashboard adds a step to every order and creates a second place where prices can be wrong. Restaurants with a single phone line and fewer than twenty calls a day will not recover the monthly base.

The strongest case is a quick service or fast casual operation with a peak-hour phone problem, an after-hours gap, or a waitlist that walks because nobody picked up. Multi-location groups get a second benefit: identical call handling at every site, which is the problem [franchise call inconsistency](/franchise-call-inconsistency/) describes. If your issue is specifically the calls that arrive after you close, [after hour call handling](/after-hour-call-handling/) covers the setup in more detail.

## Questions to put to any vendor before you sign

Ask for a live call, not a recording. Ask what happens when the integration breaks. Ask who owns the phone number.

Porting matters more than most buyers expect. If the vendor holds your main number and you leave, you need to know the number comes with you and how long the port takes. Ask for that in writing.

Then ask for the call log from a comparable restaurant — same cuisine, similar volume — with the abandoned-call rate before and after. A vendor who cannot produce one is selling you a demo. A vendor who can is selling you a phone line that works at 11pm on a Saturday, which is the actual product.

- Who owns the phone number and how long does porting take?
- What is the escalation path when the agent cannot resolve a call?
- What happens during a POS outage?
- Can I hear recordings of real calls from restaurants like mine?
- What is the notice period and is there a contract minimum?
